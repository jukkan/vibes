How a democracy falls

A single election does not make a country a “functioning democracy.” This page uses major democracy measurement frameworks to tell a hypothetical “how it starts” story for a large democracy drifting toward a Russia-like electoral autocracy—and to give you a rubric you can use to assess any country.

Entry point: “But they have elections”

Many modern autocracies keep the form of elections while removing the substance: opposition can’t campaign safely, independent media is throttled, courts and election administration are politicized, and the cost of dissent becomes unaffordable.

Regimes of the World (V-Dem) is explicit about this: it distinguishes four regime types. “Electoral autocracies” have multiparty elections for the executive, but fail minimum standards like freedom of expression/association and free & fair elections. “Liberal democracies” add strong judicial/legislative constraints and equality before the law. (V-Dem 2025 Democracy Report)

Minimum democratic control (a practical definition)

Citizens can replace leaders without fear or structural disadvantage, and governments cannot neutralize that threat.

  • Contestability: real competition and uncertainty in outcomes.
  • Participation: broad, inclusive suffrage and feasible voting.
  • Rights: speech, association, press, and due process are protected.
  • Constraints: independent courts, legislature, and oversight can stop abuses.

A fast rubric you can apply to any country

The goal is not a single magic number. It’s a repeatable way to decide where a country sits, and why. Use multiple datasets (different methods, different biases) and look for the same story.

Step 1 — Classify the regime type (broad category)

V-Dem: Regimes of the World

4 categories: Closed Autocracy, Electoral Autocracy, Electoral Democracy, Liberal Democracy. The 2025 report includes clear definitions and a per-country table for 2024.

Use for: “Is it democracy, and how deep?”

Source: V-Dem Democracy Report 2025 (see Table 1).

EIU Democracy Index

Scores countries across five dimensions (electoral process & pluralism, functioning of government, political participation, political culture, civil liberties), and groups them into regime clusters.

Use for: a well-known “single score” benchmark plus dimension scores.

Source: Our World in Data summary (method overview) + EIU 2024 table.

Step 2 — Audit the pillars (why a score moved)

1) Elections you can lose

Opposition can run; votes are counted; winners take office.

  • Independent election administration
  • No voter intimidation, no “managed outcomes”
  • Peaceful transfer of power

Metrics to watch: “clean elections”, “electoral integrity”.

2) Information freedom

Citizens can learn, criticize, and organize.

  • Independent media survives financially and legally
  • Government can’t criminalize dissent
  • Disinformation isn’t state-enforced reality

V-Dem flags freedom of expression as a frequent early casualty.

3) Rule of law & equal citizenship

Law binds rulers and protects minorities and opponents.

  • Independent courts; credible due process
  • No selective prosecution / impunity
  • Equal political rights and equal protection

Watch: judicial independence, political equality, civil liberties.

Step 3 — Cross-check with two independent index families (reduce “measurement illusion” risk)

Freedom House: Freedom in the World

Aggregates 25 indicators into Political Rights (0–40) and Civil Liberties (0–60), total 0–100. (Method section inside the 2025 report.)

Source: FITW 2025 methodology.

International IDEA: Global State of Democracy (GSoD)

A hierarchical framework across four attributes: Representation, Rights, Rule of Law, Participation, with technical procedures and codebooks.

Source: GSoD Technical Procedures Guide.

The critical pillars, and how they typically fail

Democracy rarely collapses overnight. Research on “backsliding” and “autocratization” points to a pattern of incremental moves that degrade these pillars while keeping elections as theater.

Pillar A — Elections and alternation (the ability to vote out rulers)

Healthy

Opposition can campaign, media can scrutinize, election rules are stable and impartial, and winners take office even when incumbents lose.

Failure modes

Rule changes that skew competition; harassment or disqualification of opponents; “administrative” obstacles to voting; intimidation; selective counting/validation; delegitimizing losses in advance; refusal to accept results.

Pillar B — Free information environment (speech, press, and reality-testing)

Healthy

Independent media exists at scale; journalists can investigate; citizens can organize and criticize without fear.

Failure modes

Media capture (ownership, licensing, subsidies, advertising pressure); strategic lawsuits; “foreign agent” rules; platform pressure; criminalizing protest or “disrespect”; surveillance creating chilling effects. V-Dem reports “losses in freedom of expression are alarming” and worsening in dozens of countries.

Pillar C — Constraints on executive power (courts, legislature, oversight)

Healthy

Courts and legislatures can block illegal orders; inspectors general and auditors can investigate; law enforcement remains professional.

Failure modes

“Executive aggrandizement” (legal-seeming changes concentrating power); replacing independent officials with loyalists; sidelining oversight; weaponizing prosecution; emergency powers that never sunset. (See Bermeo’s typology of backsliding.)

Pillar D — Equal citizenship and civil liberties

Healthy

Equal political rights; minority protection; due process; non-discrimination; no collective punishment.

Failure modes

Making rights conditional (ethnicity, religion, loyalty); using policing/surveillance selectively; mass administrative coercion; restricting education and scientific institutions to enforce ideology.

The “how it starts” scenario (fictional, but mapped to measurable criteria)

Below is a theoretical sequence of events that would plausibly push a large democracy down the same measurement channels that distinguish a liberal democracy from an electoral autocracy. Think of it as a stress test: If X happened, which pillar fails first?

1

Information capture becomes financially “normal”

Ultra-wealthy donors and aligned firms consolidate mainstream media and the ad-tech money pipeline. It looks like “business efficiency,” but it changes the feasible range of criticism and investigation.

Index impact: press freedom + freedom of expression indicators decline → civic space shrinks.

2

Rules shift from persuasion to enforcement

Executive orders push boundaries that used to be career-ending; courts and congress stop acting as effective brakes. “Legal” becomes the primary cover story.

Index impact: executive constraints weaken; rule of law and judicial independence degrade.

3

Surveillance + selective punishment changes incentives

A broad surveillance machine (online + facial recognition) is built; “speech” is still nominally legal, but the expected cost of dissent rises sharply (job loss, legal harassment, travel restrictions).

Index impact: association, protest, and civil liberties indicators fall; “fear” replaces debate.

4

Equal citizenship becomes negotiable

Targeted questioning of citizenship rights and mass-scale deportation machinery becomes normalized. This does two things: (1) it narrows who can safely participate; (2) it signals that rights are conditional.

Index impact: political equality + civil liberties collapse; participation becomes stratified.

5

Science and education become ideology enforcement

Public institutions that create shared reality (universities, public health, national statistics, curricula) are attacked or captured. Book restrictions become a proxy war for “who controls the future voters.”

Index impact: deliberative quality and academic freedom proxies decline; polarization + misinformation become structural.

6

Elections remain — but democracy doesn’t

Elections continue, but the contest is no longer fair: opposition is constrained, the information environment is warped, and losing becomes too dangerous for incumbents.

Regime outcome: this is the path toward electoral autocracy — the “elections-as-window-dressing” regime type explicitly described by RoW.

Global context: V-Dem reports that 72% of the world’s population lives in autocracies (about 5.8 billion people), and that liberal democracies host less than 12% (~0.9 billion). These are not marginal phenomena. (V-Dem 2025)

Money and oligarchs: the “Access All Areas” pass

Money can be a powerful accelerator because it buys control over chokepoints: media, legal defense/offense, patronage networks, and access to decision-makers. But in most backsliding research, money is rarely the only cause — it becomes decisive when it aligns with institutional weakness and polarization.

What money can do (high leverage moves)

  • Media capture: buy outlets, starve competitors, shape the agenda.
  • Rule-making access: regulatory capture, private “drafting” of laws, procurement favoritism.
  • Legal asymmetry: fund strategic litigation, intimidate watchdogs, finance compliance costs for allies.
  • Political gatekeeping: bankroll primary challenges, fund parallel campaign ecosystems.

Related concepts: “state capture” (systematically shaping rules/decisions for narrow interests). See classic state capture framing and modern “media capture” work.

What money usually can’t do alone

  • Replace courts and enforcement without a loyal bureaucracy.
  • Prevent electoral defeat if elections remain clean and outcomes are accepted.
  • Stop civic mobilization if speech/association are robust and fear is low.

Empirically, democratic decline is often explained by combinations of: executive aggrandizement, strategic electoral manipulation, shrinking civic space, and information control. Money amplifies each, but institutions decide whether it “sticks.”

Data point worth remembering: V-Dem highlights “losses in freedom of expression” worsening in 44 countries by 2024. If oligarchic capture controls media and speech enforcement, this pillar can fall early — and everything else becomes easier. (V-Dem 2025)
